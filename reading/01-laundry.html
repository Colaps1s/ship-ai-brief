Your laundry is harder than chess

9 min read · Research catch-up: 2024-10-31 · Written 15 October 2026

Original cover artwork. Research images and data are linked in the source.

The next time you leave a T-shirt inside out on the floor, consider what you have just done to a robot's afternoon.

The shirt has no fixed shape. One sleeve may be hiding under the body. The fabric changes as soon as it is touched. The robot cannot simply remember where the corners were a second ago and assume they are still there. To finish the job, it needs to keep looking, make a move, notice the result, and adjust.

You do most of this without describing any of it to yourself. That is why a machine doing laundry can look less impressive than a machine winning chess, even though the laundry exposes a much messier engineering problem.

This is the story behind pi-zero, a robot policy introduced by Physical Intelligence in October 2024. It is also a useful way to watch almost any robot demonstration without either dismissing it or falling for the sales pitch.

What the researchers actually showed

Physical Intelligence reported training a general-purpose robot model on data from different tasks and robot platforms. The project combined a vision-language model with a system for producing continuous robot actions. Its demonstrations included folding laundry, sorting items from a table and assembling cardboard boxes. Some complicated tasks used additional task-specific training. The company published examples of failures as well as successes.

These were research demonstrations, not proof that a domestic robot could handle an unfamiliar home all day. The researchers themselves identified robustness, safety and longer sequences of work as unfinished problems. We have not tested the system.

That distinction matters. A robot can do something genuinely new without being a product you should expect to buy next week.

A shirt refuses to be a chess piece

Think about a chessboard after a move. The pieces occupy a small set of permitted squares. Their identities are stable. A knight stays a knight. The board does not sag when the machine picks up a pawn. A legal move has a precise meaning, and the result is easy to represent in software.

Now imagine representing the shirt on your bed. You could record a photograph, but a photograph does not directly tell you which layer is on top, how much friction there is, or whether the next tug will reveal a sleeve or tighten a knot. You could record the position of every visible patch of fabric, but much of the shirt may be hidden. The scene you need to act on is partly unknown.

This comparison is not a claim that every laundry task is computationally harder than every chess problem. It is an explanation of why everyday physical competence does not follow automatically from impressive performance on a board or a screen.

The world supplies an enormous number of details that game designers usually tidy away. People supply the missing competence through experience: how gently to pull, where to place a hand, when to stop and look again. A robot needs some way of acquiring those useful habits.

The interesting moment is the second attempt

A perfect fold is pleasing to watch. A botched fold followed by a sensible recovery can tell you more.

Suppose a robot reaches for the corner of a towel and catches the middle instead. There are at least three possible reactions. It can continue the original routine and make a mess. It can stop and ask a person to reset the scene. Or it can notice that the situation has changed and choose a different action.

Only the third looks much like the everyday usefulness we want from a household machine. The first may be a beautifully choreographed demonstration. The second may still be useful in a carefully managed workplace. But neither should be confused with a robot that reliably takes responsibility for finishing the chore.

This is why recovery is a better viewing habit than applause. Watch what happens after an object slips. Watch whether the machine can resume after an interruption. Watch whether the environment quietly gets reset between clips. None of those observations requires you to know the model's name.

The example here is a thought experiment, not a report of an additional test of pi-zero. It shows the sort of evidence that would make a demonstration more informative.

A robot has more than one boss

When a person asks a robot to clear a table, the visible request sounds simple. The actual job contains several different decisions.

It needs to decide what counts as rubbish, what should be kept, what can be stacked and where each thing belongs. It also needs to turn those decisions into physical movements. A sensible plan is not enough if the gripper crushes the cup. Excellent gripping is not enough if it efficiently throws away your glasses.

This separation is useful outside robotics too. People often treat an AI system's fluent explanation as evidence that it can complete the entire task. But explaining what should happen and making it happen are different abilities. A machine might describe a fold beautifully and still be unable to locate a sleeve.

The reverse mistake is possible as well. A robot may perform a movement competently without being able to give a satisfying explanation of it. For practical use, we need to judge the complete job, not insist that every component behaves like a conversational assistant.

The useful question is therefore specific: which decisions are handled by the machine, which are supplied by a person, and which are avoided by arranging the scene in advance?

A neat table can hide a lot of work

Here is another hypothetical demonstration. Two robots both place a mug in a cupboard. One starts with the mug upright on a marked square, the cupboard open and the destination shelf empty. The other starts with an unfamiliar mug among dishes, a closed cupboard and a shelf that may already be crowded.

Both videos can honestly be titled "robot puts away a mug." They demonstrate very different amounts of useful independence.

That does not make the first system fraudulent. Constraining a task is often exactly how useful automation begins. A machine that does one repetitive job in a stable setting can be valuable. The problem appears when a narrow setup is presented as evidence for an unrestricted household helper.

For a viewer, the easiest habit is to mentally keep the starting conditions attached to the result. Do not let a glamorous final action erase everything that made it possible.

An edited highlight is evidence that the action occurred. It is much weaker evidence about how often it occurs, how much assistance it needs or how it behaves when the room changes.

Why different examples matter

Imagine teaching someone to fold a towel by showing the same towel in the same position a thousand times. They might become very good at that exact arrangement. They might still hesitate when handed a tiny face cloth or a large, damp bath towel.

Experience becomes more useful when it includes meaningful variety. Different objects, positions and outcomes give the learner a chance to discover what can be reused. In robotics, the hope behind generalist systems is that learning across tasks can produce abilities that travel further than a single rehearsed routine.

But "trained on many things" is not the same as "ready for anything." Some experiences transfer well and others do not. A robot that has handled a soft shirt has not thereby demonstrated that it can safely handle a sleeping pet, an electrical cable or a hot saucepan.

The right response to a broader training recipe is curiosity about the next test. What unfamiliar object did it manage? What changed in the room? What still required extra examples? A list of training ingredients is a reason to investigate, not a substitute for the results.

Useful does not have to mean human-shaped

Our imagination tends to put a general-purpose robot in the shape of a person. That is understandable: homes were arranged for human bodies. But the useful part of a chore is the outcome, not the silhouette of the worker.

A machine that folds one kind of item in a back room could be more valuable than an impressive humanoid that needs constant supervision. A fixed pair of arms could solve a worthwhile problem without ever walking upstairs. A specialized appliance could remove a chore altogether.

This is our assessment, not a claim about a commercial pi-zero product. The broader point is that the route to helpful machines may look more ordinary than the demonstrations that attract the most attention.

There is a practical design question hiding here. Would you rather have a spectacular machine that occasionally gets everything right, or an unremarkable machine that consistently removes one unpleasant job? People buying tools usually care about the second. People sharing videos are often drawn to the first.

The boring questions make the exciting result credible

If you watch another laundry robot video, try asking five things. Was the task adapted to the robot? Is this a complete attempt or an edited sequence? What counts as success? How much human help is outside the frame? What happens when the machine makes a small mistake?

Those questions do not require cynicism. They make it easier to recognize real progress. A system that clearly explains its limits gives you something concrete to compare with the next one.

The genuinely striking prospect is not that a robot can imitate the final gesture of folding. It is that a machine might gradually acquire the ability to cope with an object that will not sit still, will not keep its shape and will not politely reveal all the information the machine needs.

For humans, that is laundry. For robotics, it is a window into why the physical world is such a demanding teacher.
