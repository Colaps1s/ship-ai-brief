What if you could walk into an AI video?

Genie 3 generates an interactive world as you move through it. Turn around, explore, see what happens next.

The hard part is remembering what was behind you. DeepMind's 2025 demo kept worlds consistent for minutes, at 720p and 24 frames per second.

Still a research demo: limited duration, imperfect actions and trouble with details like text.

Imagine opening a door in a video and deciding to walk through it.

Usually, a video cannot oblige. The camera goes where the person filming went. You can pause or rewind, but the scene has already happened. A game can respond because someone built a world with rules, objects and possible actions.

Genie 3 explored a different possibility: generating an environment that changes as you navigate it. The next view is produced in response to what you do, which makes a generated scene feel less like a clip and more like a place.

That is an exciting idea. It also invites a particularly slippery misunderstanding: a world that looks continuous is not necessarily a world that behaves like a conventional game engine underneath.

The announcement, kept in its original year

Google DeepMind announced Genie 3 on 5 August 2025. It described prompt-generated environments that could be navigated in real time at 24 frames per second and 720p, with consistency over several minutes. Its examples included natural settings and imagined scenes. The announcement also described limits involving interaction, multiple agents, text rendering and the duration of a reliable experience.

Those are the developer's reported capabilities and limitations at announcement. They are not our hands-on benchmark or a statement of current access, price or product limits in October 2026.

This article is about the idea behind that demonstration: what changes when a generated image becomes something you can steer?

A door can be a picture or a promise

In a photograph, a painted door has no obligation to open. In a game, a door may be an object with an attached rule: press a button, play an animation, reveal another space. In a generated interactive scene, the relationship can be less explicit.

You may approach what looks like a doorway and expect an interior. The system then needs to produce a plausible continuation. The visual suggestion has become an invitation to act.

That shift is important. A small inconsistency in a still image may pass unnoticed. The same inconsistency becomes much harder to hide when a viewer can walk around it, return to it or try to use it.

Imagine a staircase that looks convincing from below but cannot support a coherent journey upstairs. Or a courtyard whose far wall changes when you turn away. These are hypothetical examples, not additional tests we performed. They illustrate the demands that interaction places on an image generator.

The scene has to answer not just "what might look right here?" but "what should happen after the action the viewer just chose?"

Why turning around is such a revealing action

When you enter a real room, the room remains behind you. You do not have to keep looking at the sofa to prevent it from being replaced by a piano.

That persistence feels so ordinary that we rarely notice it. But it is one of the reasons interactive generation is harder than producing an isolated attractive view. The system needs to keep some useful relationship between what it showed before and what it shows now.

In a conventional game, a sofa can remain an object in a stored scene even when it is outside the camera's view. In a generated world, there may be a different representation of the history that helps produce future frames. The outward experience can look similar while the machinery is very different.

This does not mean generated worlds are useless until they perfectly reproduce conventional engines. It means we should judge them on the kind of continuity they actually provide. A dreamlike exploration can be compelling even when it would be unsuitable for a puzzle requiring precise object permanence.

Beauty and reliability are different design materials

Imagine two experiences. In one, every corridor leads to another extraordinary landscape. The details drift, but the movement feels like wandering through a dream. In the other, the graphics are plain, yet a key placed in a drawer is still there an hour later and every puzzle behaves predictably.

The first might be a delightful art toy. The second might be a better mystery game. Neither is improved simply by pretending to be the other.

For creators, this is a useful way to think about generative media. A limitation can suggest a form rather than only obstruct it. Unstable worlds might suit surreal exploration, short performances or visual brainstorming. A tightly specified training simulation requires a different standard.

These are possible uses, not claims that Genie 3 already fulfills them reliably. The point is that the question "is this ready?" needs an object. Ready for a two-minute imaginative stroll is not the same as ready to teach someone a safety-critical procedure.

A world model is not automatically a physics textbook

The term "world model" can sound as though a system has acquired a comprehensive internal theory of reality. In practice, we need to ask which relationships it represents well enough to support a particular task.

A model might generate water that looks persuasive while still failing a precise fluid-dynamics test. It might show a plausible falling object without being a trustworthy source of measurements. Looking familiar and being quantitatively correct are different achievements.

This matters because our eyes are accustomed to treating video as evidence that a physical event occurred. A generated sequence changes that relationship. The camera can appear to show an experiment that never happened.

For entertainment, that freedom can be wonderful. For scientific claims, it means the images need an accompanying method and evaluation. A beautiful simulated scene does not become a measurement because it resembles footage.

Our own covers follow the same rule: they illustrate an idea. They do not document the research system's output.

The game designer's job changes shape

If a creator can propose a setting in ordinary language, some early visual exploration becomes easier to imagine. Instead of beginning with a blank editor, they could begin by trying possibilities: a rainy station, an impossible garden, a house built around a tree.

But a setting is only part of a game. There are goals, feedback, pacing, controls, consequences and reasons to return. A memorable place can support those things; it does not automatically supply them.

Imagine a murder mystery whose architecture shifts whenever the player turns around. That might be an intentional mechanic. If it is accidental, it could make clues meaningless. Imagine a racing game whose road changes in ways the player cannot anticipate. That might be exciting for a moment and frustrating over time.

The distinction is authorship. Designers decide which surprises are enjoyable and which break the agreement with the player. More generative freedom makes that decision more important, not less.

This is why a playable scene and a finished game should remain separate labels. The first can be an impressive new ingredient without claiming to replace everything required for the second.

The simulator question is even harder

Interactive worlds could also be useful for exploring how an AI agent acts. A simulated environment can provide many situations without physically rebuilding a room for every attempt.

But training in a simulation creates another question: what did the agent learn about the real task, and what did it learn about the simulator? A system can become excellent at exploiting regularities that do not exist outside its training environment.

This issue is not unique to generated worlds. It is a general challenge whenever practice and deployment differ. A flight simulator, a video game and a laboratory setup all make choices about what to represent and what to omit.

Generated environments could expand the range of practice. That possibility does not remove the need to test whether the resulting skills transfer. Variety is useful when it teaches the relationships that matter, not merely when it produces more visually different scenes.

The crucial evidence would therefore include what happens after training, on a task the simulator cannot quietly redefine to make the agent look successful.

Try imagining the experience before predicting the industry

Before asking whether world models will replace games, picture a smaller question. What would you enjoy doing for five minutes in a place that responds to you but may not remain perfectly stable?

You might explore an impossible building, sketch the mood of a story or simply see where a path goes. Someone else might find the same experience aimless. That is a useful disagreement because it concerns the actual form, not a distant claim about an entire industry.

The interesting development is the new relationship between image and action. A generated scene no longer has to be only something you watch. It can become something whose next moment partly depends on you.

Whether that develops into a tool, a game, a research environment or a medium we do not yet have a comfortable name for is an open question. The 2025 announcement showed one step in that direction. It did not settle the destination.
