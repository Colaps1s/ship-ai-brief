The answer looked brilliant. The checker still said no.

8 min read · Research catch-up: 2024-07-25 · Written 15 October 2026

Original cover artwork. Research images and data are linked in the source.

A fluent explanation can be wrong in a way that is difficult to notice. Mathematics offers a particularly demanding alternative: a proof whose steps can be checked against explicit rules.

That difference is the interesting part of AlphaProof. The story is not merely that an AI produced impressive mathematical text. It is that a system searched for arguments in a formal language where a checker could reject steps that did not follow.

In July 2024, Google DeepMind reported that AlphaProof and AlphaGeometry 2 together solved four of the six problems from that year's International Mathematical Olympiad at a silver-medal-equivalent score. The result came with important differences from the conditions faced by the students.

Both parts belong in the story: the achievement and the conditions that make it interpretable.

What was reported

The 25 July 2024 announcement reported 28 points out of 42 under the IMO scoring scheme. People manually translated the competition problems into formal language. The systems took from minutes to as much as three days, unlike the students' timed sessions. AlphaProof used the Lean formal language; AlphaGeometry 2 handled the geometry problem. Two combinatorics problems remained unsolved.

This was silver-medal-standard performance under the reported evaluation, not an official student medal won under identical conditions. It did not establish that every mathematical question could now be solved or that ordinary chatbot explanations were automatically reliable.

The distinction is central to understanding what the work contributed.

Finding an answer and checking an argument are different jobs

Suppose someone hands you a completed crossword. You may be able to check each entry against the clues more easily than you could have produced every answer from a blank grid.

Mathematical proof checking is not the same task, but the analogy introduces a useful separation. Generating a candidate can be difficult. Verifying that a candidate satisfies a precise set of rules can sometimes be more tractable.

That separation creates a productive partnership. A search process proposes possible steps; a checker constrains which steps count as valid. Rejected proposals are not failures of the whole idea. They are part of the search.

The resulting system does not need every proposed continuation to be correct. It needs a route toward a final result that survives the relevant verification.

This is a different standard from asking whether an answer sounds plausible to a casual reader.

Formal language makes the hidden assumptions visible

In ordinary conversation, we leave many things implicit. People can often infer what a speaker intended. Mathematical arguments also rely on shared understanding, but a formal proof assistant demands that particular relationships be expressed in a form its rules can check.

That precision can feel laborious. It is also the source of the benefit. A step cannot pass merely because it resembles a familiar proof or uses impressive vocabulary.

Imagine writing instructions for a very literal colleague who will not accept "and obviously this follows" unless the required connection has actually been supplied. That colleague might be frustrating. They could also catch a gap everyone else stepped over.

The analogy is simplified, but it explains why a formal checker changes the evidence behind an AI-generated argument. It supplies a separate constraint on the candidate rather than relying entirely on the generator's confidence.

The translation step matters

A checker can verify a formal statement while leaving open whether that statement matches the original human problem. If the translation changes an assumption, the machine could prove something valid but different from what was intended.

This is why the announcement's manual formalization detail is important. It identifies work people supplied before the systems tackled the problems. It also reminds us that moving between ordinary language and formal representations is a substantive part of the process.

There is a familiar parallel in software. A program can correctly implement the wrong specification. Passing a test does not help if the test describes a different requirement from the one the user meant.

The formal proof is strong evidence about the formal statement under its rules and assumptions. Understanding the broader result still requires examining the complete chain, including how the problem entered that formal setting.

Why the time comparison is not a footnote

The IMO is a timed human competition. A system that takes days on some problems is operating under different conditions from a student working through a fixed exam session.

That does not make the system's solutions uninteresting. It changes the comparison being made. We can recognize a difficult problem solved while also declining to pretend that every condition was the same.

This is useful well beyond mathematics. AI evaluations often compare systems that receive different amounts of time, tools, context or human preparation. Those differences may be reasonable for the research question. They need to remain visible when interpreting the score.

An honest account can therefore say both that the result reached a particular scoring standard and that it did so under its own reported conditions. The extra precision gives the achievement a clear shape.

The checker is powerful because the task is constrained

Formal mathematical systems provide unusually explicit standards. Many ordinary problems do not.

If an assistant proposes a good holiday, a kind reply or a compelling design, there may be no compact checker that determines the answer for everyone. Preferences, context and consequences can be difficult to formalize.

That means we should not expect a proof-checking success to transfer automatically to every domain where a chatbot gives an explanation. The verification mechanism is part of what makes the result possible.

At the same time, the pattern is worth noticing. Wherever a task has a trustworthy external check, a system may be able to use that check to improve its search. The challenge is determining whether the check measures the thing we actually care about.

A reliable checker of the wrong property can still encourage the wrong result.

A little puzzle about convincing explanations

Imagine two fictional assistants answering a difficult question. The first gives a beautiful explanation and says it is certain. The second offers a less elegant result accompanied by a record that an independent checker can verify.

Which would you trust more? The answer depends on whether the checker is appropriate, whether its assumptions match the question and whether the result really passed it. But those are concrete things to investigate. The first assistant's tone gives you far less to inspect.

Now imagine the first assistant writes its own checker after seeing its own answer. The evidence may become weaker, especially if the checker quietly encodes the same mistake.

These are hypothetical examples. They illustrate why independence and task fit matter in evaluation. An extra check is useful when it adds a meaningful constraint, not merely another confident-looking output.

Mathematical creativity still has room inside the constraints

A formal system can sound like a machine that only follows obvious steps. But searching for a useful sequence of steps can itself be demanding.

The existence of clear rules does not make every successful argument easy to find. Chess has clear rules too. So do many puzzles whose solutions feel inventive when someone discovers them.

This is part of the appeal of the research. A system can explore possible arguments while a separate mechanism checks validity. The constraint does not eliminate the search; it gives the search a standard to satisfy.

For a reader, that is a more interesting picture than either "the AI understands everything" or "it is merely following rules." Powerful behavior can emerge from a system whose parts play different roles.

The generator proposes. The checker pushes back. The result has to survive both.

What remains after the medal headline

The memorable comparison was with a silver medal. The enduring idea is the combination of learned search and explicit verification.

It suggests a useful way to judge AI systems generally: ask what constrains the answer after it is generated. Is there a measurement, a formal check, a test on independent examples or a human review capable of identifying the relevant errors?

The answer will differ by task. Not everything can become a theorem. But the question helps us distinguish an attractive output from evidence that the output satisfies a meaningful requirement.

The 2024 result did not make mistakes disappear. It showed one setting in which a machine could search for difficult answers while being held to a standard stronger than sounding right.

For anyone who has ever been persuaded by a polished but mistaken explanation, that is a useful development to understand.
