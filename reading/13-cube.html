The robot knew the moves. Holding the cube was harder.

7 min read · Research catch-up: 2019-10-15 · Written 15 October 2026

Original cover artwork. Research images and data are linked in the source.

For a computer, choosing a sequence of moves to solve a Rubik's Cube is one problem. Getting a mechanical hand to carry out those moves without dropping the cube is another.

The distinction turns an ordinary puzzle into a revealing robotics experiment. A person solving a cube constantly adjusts the grip, rotates the object and recovers from tiny slips. The hands are doing a great deal of work that the written solution does not mention.

In October 2019, OpenAI showed a human-like robot hand manipulating and solving a cube. The researchers' main challenge was dexterous control and transfer from simulation to physical hardware. A separate algorithm supplied the solution moves.

That detail makes the demonstration more interesting, because it identifies the part that was genuinely difficult.

What the original project reported

OpenAI's 15 October 2019 announcement described neural networks trained in simulation and transferred to a real robot hand using automatic domain randomization. Kociemba's algorithm selected the puzzle solution steps. The learned control system handled physical manipulation. Reported success varied with scramble difficulty, and dropping the cube or reaching a timeout counted as failure. The project also tested disturbances and described limitations.

This was a research system for a particular manipulation task. It was not a general household robot, and the learned hand controller was not independently discovering the mathematical cube-solving algorithm.

Keeping the components separate helps explain the achievement.

The instructions leave out the fingers

Imagine a recipe that says, "crack an egg." To a person who cooks, that is a manageable instruction. To a machine, it conceals questions about grip, force, contact, orientation and what happens when the shell breaks unexpectedly.

The cube has a similar hidden layer. A sequence of face rotations can be written down neatly. Performing those rotations with a multi-jointed hand requires the object to remain supported while fingers move around it.

A small error changes the next situation. The cube may sit at a slightly different angle. The grip may be less stable. Continuing the intended movement without noticing the difference can make the error grow.

This is why a physical demonstration can matter even when the high-level puzzle already has a known solution. The robot is confronting the messy translation between a symbolic instruction and an object that can slip.

A simulation is a place to practice imperfectly

Training in simulation has an obvious attraction. A virtual hand can attempt actions without the same physical wear, reset effort and practical constraints as a real mechanism.

The difficulty is that the simulation is not the real hand. Its friction, delays, contact behavior and other properties can differ. A controller that becomes excellent at one perfectly fixed virtual setup may be poorly prepared for the actual hardware.

The project's randomization approach addressed that mismatch by making practice conditions vary. Rather than learning only one exact simulated world, the controller encountered a range of conditions whose difficulty could expand as training progressed.

The intuition is familiar. If you learn to carry a cup only when it is always the same weight and always placed in the same spot, your skill may be fragile. Variation can encourage adjustments that work across a wider range of situations.

That is an analogy, not a full technical description of the training method. It explains why changing the practice environment can be part of making an eventual physical action more robust.

Recovery is a kind of knowledge

A machine that executes a flawless routine can be impressive. A machine that notices a small disruption and continues can reveal a different ability.

Think of walking while carrying a bag. You do not normally calculate every step in advance and then execute the plan regardless of what happens. Your movement keeps responding to the world.

The same broad distinction matters in manipulation. Does the controller only reproduce a narrow path, or can it adjust when the object and its own grip differ from expectation?

The 2019 project included robustness tests, but their existence should not be generalized into a claim that the robot could handle every disturbance. They make the question concrete: which variations were tested, and how did performance change?

An honest failure in that testing can be more informative than another perfect highlight. It shows where the learned behavior still runs out.

The solve rate belongs beside the video

A video of one successful solve establishes that the system can complete that attempt. It does not establish how often it succeeds.

The announcement described different success rates for different scramble difficulties. That distinction prevents a single polished demonstration from standing in for the entire performance distribution.

It also shows why the definition of failure matters. If someone puts a dropped cube back into the hand and the robot continues, that may be a useful recovery demonstration. It is different from an uninterrupted successful attempt under a scoring rule that counts the drop as failure.

Neither observation needs to be hidden. Clear definitions let readers understand both the capability and the remaining problem.

The useful habit is to ask for the denominator. How many attempts were made under the reported conditions, and what was counted as success? A result without that context is easier to admire than to compare.

Useful intelligence can be a team of different methods

It is tempting to think that a more impressive AI system should use one grand model for everything. The cube project points to another possibility: different methods can handle different parts of the problem.

A known algorithm can select the logical sequence. A learned controller can manage the physical movements. A perception system can help estimate the object's state. The overall result depends on their interaction.

This kind of division is ordinary in engineering. We do not usually demand that a camera, a motor and a navigation system all use the same internal method before calling a vehicle useful.

The important question is whether the complete system does the job and whether each component's contribution is described honestly. A hybrid design can be a strength rather than an admission of defeat.

For a reader, identifying the components also makes a future improvement easier to interpret. Did the system learn a better plan, see more accurately or execute the same plan with fewer physical mistakes?

A cube is a wonderfully unforgiving object

The task is visually understandable. Most readers can recognize the goal, notice a dropped cube and appreciate the difference between a clumsy turn and a controlled one.

That makes it a useful public demonstration. It brings a difficult control problem into a form that does not require a specialist to understand why success is demanding.

At the same time, the cube remains a particular object with particular mechanics. Success does not automatically demonstrate the ability to handle everything else a person can hold. A soft object, a fragile object or an unfamiliar tool creates different demands.

The result is a step along a path, not the whole path compressed into a colorful toy.

That is enough to make it worth revisiting. Much of the difficulty of useful physical AI lives in the ordinary movements people stop noticing because they perform them so easily.

What the hand teaches us

The most revealing sentence in the story may be that the solution algorithm was already available. The hard part was making the hand do what the solution required in the real world.

That reverses a common intuition about AI. Tasks that look intellectually demanding can become tractable when their rules are explicit. Everyday actions can remain difficult because they depend on feedback, contact and adaptation.

The same contrast appears when a robot folds a shirt or clears a table. The final action looks simple because people bring a lifetime of physical experience to it.

A cube-solving hand makes that invisible experience briefly visible. The machine's awkwardness reminds us how much competence is packed into an ordinary grip.
